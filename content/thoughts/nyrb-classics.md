*---NYRB?*

[New York Review Books](https://www.nyrb.com) is the publishing
house of the literary journal and magazine, [*The New York Review
of Books*](https://www.nybooks.com), of which I proudly know
nothing about. However, their publishing branch is excellent, in
my uneducated opinion. Their main imprint is NYRB Classics, which
publishes "an eclectic mix of fiction and nonfiction from
different eras and times and of various sorts."[^1] Many of the
books are either out of print or rarely translated into English,
ripe for reprinting.

If you've been around a particular side of YouTube, you might
have heard praise for John Williams' *Stoner* or seen shelves
stacked with rows of solid, colorful books. That corner is where,
more than three years ago, I heard about the NYRB Book Club, a
yearly subscription that offers one new book from the Classics
catalog each month. It is not a crazy steal, the books just
slightly discounted, but the variety appealed to me. I am not
a worldly, literary kind. I read "classics" and enjoy them.
That's the extent of it. What better way to force myself to
take a break from fantastical worlds and read new stuff than
a random literary volume dropped off in the mailbox once a month?

Two renewals later, thirty-six books. In total, ten made
four or five stars. Only one earned five---Lisa Tuttle's *My
Death*. It deserves a more careful review, and I plan to re-read
it sometime-maybe-soon.

I was going to write a short review for each of them; I have
since changed my mind. With my horrible memory, there was never a
chance. Instead, here are my thoughts on two that stuck the most.

### ***Friday***, Michel Tournier
- Originally Published 1967

A retelling of the classic story of Robinson Crusoe. I've never
read Defoe's original work, so this is my first introduction to
the famous story. At first compelled to civilize the island
with technology, agriculture, social structure (even for one
person), and religion, Crusoe abandons the effort in favor of some
kind of natural paganism when he notices Friday's carefree nature.

I found the philosophical digressions in Crusoe's journal
extremely interesting. My favorite was the discussion on how we
have a prejudice against breadth in favor of depth. We interpret
"superficial" to mean lacking depth rather than possessing great
breadth. Why should we love deeply rather than widely?

I feel a little guilty for reading this before Defoe's original.
But oh well, at least when I do get around to it, I'll have a
little extra enthusiasm.

> Since I have been here I have become something of a specialist
in silence, or in silences, I should say...There are airy, scented
silences like a June night in England, others with the glaucous
thickness of the mire, and yet others that are hard and sonorous
as ebony.

### ***The Universal Baseball Association Inc.***, Robert Coover
- Originally Published 1968

I have never played or followed baseball. I couldn't describe how
the game is played. First opening this book and reading the title,
I shelved it in my brain beside the rest of the NYRBs that just
weren't *me*. Fortunately for me, this book isn't really about
baseball. Not exactly.

Enter Henry, dead-end accountant on the brink of losing his job
because he can't stop playing his simulated baseball game. And
if you're like me, and your first thought is bets and trades and
ridiculous punishments, you'd be far from the truth. When this
book was written, the idea of fantasy sports didn't exist as we
know it. What Henry has created is an entire alternate world:
The Universal Baseball Association. For 55 in-game seasons,
Henry has simulated not just the game, but the league and the
politics of baseball, with dice, scorecards, fictional records,
characters, and more. Coover's writing brings the players to
life, and the way the real and unreal blend together shows just
how obsessed Henry is.

One of my favorite passages is when Henry invites his only friend,
Lou, to play a few rounds of the game. Greasy pizza fingers
on the scorecards, split beer, and a heretical disrespect for
the association make for an uncomfortable night for Henry. It
perfectly captures the feeling of trying to share a niche
personal obsession with a friend, when everything once magical
seems lame in the moment.

The book opens at a rough time for the UBA. Henry blames himself
for the stagnation of the league, hoping that one star pitcher,
Damon Rutherford, might garner new seasons of vitality. However,
when an incredible series of rolls leads to Damon's death by
a stray pitch, Henry loses it all. By the end of the book, he
has started to manipulate the game out of desperation. Reading
this today, you can't help but think about all the different
simulations we escape to.

> Henry had invented for them a variation on Monopoly, using
twelve, sixteen, or twenty-four boards at once and an unlimited
number of players, which opened up the possibility of wars run by
industrial giants with investments on several boards at once, the
buying off of whole governments ... strikes and rebellions by the
slumdwellers between "Go" and "Jail," revolutionary subversion and
sabotage with sympathetic ties across the boards ... but it never
caught on.

## Final Thoughts

I got my last book this May and didn't renew. Having to read
twelve specific books each year became too much of a commitment
and a restriction on my reading choices. If I were to join the
club again, I would spread it over two years instead of trying
to keep the pace. Anyway, nobody made me renew every year and
treat it like an assignment. It's not much of a complaint that
there are too many books!

Whether or not you can keep up, the quality and variety of the
selection is wonderful. It gave me the chance to read books
in unfamiliar genres and from unknown authors, and I'm happy
to have discovered a handful of new favorites. Best of all,
I have this amazing band of color on my shelf, a reminder to
always be as broad as I can in my reading.
